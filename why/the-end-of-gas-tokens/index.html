The end of gas tokens

Live on testnet Simplifies the whitepaper, Principle 2

To send $100 of USDT on Ethereum or Tron, you first have to buy ETH or TRX: a coin you do not want, bought only to pay a toll. On Sequentia you pay the fee in the dollars you are sending.

The toll booth

Almost every chain built for tokens works the same way. The chain has its own coin, and the protocol accepts fees in that coin and nothing else. It does not matter that the dollars in your wallet are worth exactly a dollar and are accepted everywhere: to move them you need the coin.

So a person who only ever wanted to hold dollars ends up doing three things instead of one:

  1. buying the chain’s coin on an exchange,
  2. keeping a balance of it, and watching its price,
  3. topping it up whenever it runs out, with one more transaction each time.

Chains that avoid inventing a coin usually replace it with a pegged bitcoin: L-BTC on Liquid, rBTC on Rootstock. That is the same toll with a different sticker, plus a custodian. The pegged coin exists only as long as whoever holds the real bitcoin behind it stays honest, online and unhacked.

Sequentia the stablecoin itself, or any other asset you hold nothing else

Pay in what you are sending

On Sequentia a transaction can pay its fee in any asset issued on the chain: dollars, euros, gold, a tokenised stock. There is no asset the protocol prefers. Block producers decide for themselves which assets they are willing to be paid in, and they have every reason to say yes to anything with a real market price, because fees are their whole income.

That is all a user ever sees: the wallet shows the fee in the asset being sent and what it is worth in their own currency, and the payment goes out. One asset in the wallet is enough to use the chain.

Go deeperHow a producer compares a fee in dollars with a fee in gold

When every fee is paid in one coin, choosing the best-paying transactions is arithmetic. When fees arrive in different assets it is not: a thousand units of one token and a thousand of another are not comparable quantities.

So the comparison is made on value rather than quantity. Each producer keeps a price list with one rate per asset. The value of a fee is the quantity offered multiplied by the rate of its asset, and the producer ranks transactions by value per unit of transaction weight, exactly as a Bitcoin node ranks them by satoshis per virtual byte.

The scale those values sit on is an abstract unit of account. It is a unit of measure and not an asset: nobody issues it, nobody can hold it, and no issued asset is defined to be it. It exists so that a node’s thresholds (the minimum fee it relays, the minimum it includes in a block, the increment a replacement must add) can each be a single figure.

Source: whitepaper, section 2.2. Implementation: doc/sequentia/02-open-fee-market.md.

“What stops me from paying with a worthless token?”

Nothing forbids it, and nothing obliges anyone to take it. A producer’s price list is also its acceptance list: an asset it has not priced is worth nothing on its scale, so a transaction paying in that asset never beats one paying in something real, and in practice is not carried at all.

The result is a market, not a rule. An asset with a price and with liquidity gets accepted by many producers and confirms quickly. A thinly traded one is accepted by few, and has to offer more. An asset nobody wants is, for fee purposes, simply not money.

Go deeperThree separate things decide what happens to your transaction

Only the first is a rule of the network.

  • Consensus is what every node enforces on a block. It checks only that the fee a transaction declares was really paid, in whatever asset. It never asks what the fee was worth. A transaction paying in an asset a node has never heard of is perfectly valid once it is in a block.
  • Node policy decides what one node does with a transaction that is not yet in a block: whether to keep it and pass it on. A node that does not price an asset does not relay transactions paying in it.
  • Producer policy decides which of the transactions a producer holds go into the block it builds.

Refusing an asset is therefore never a veto over the network. If some producer somewhere prices it, that producer will include the transaction and every node will accept the block. What a narrow acceptance list costs is reach: relaying is hop by hop, so a transaction can fail to arrive at the producer that would have taken it. This is why a wallet asks its own node whether it accepts an asset before offering it as a fee.

Source: whitepaper, section 2.3.

If nobody takes your fee, change it

A transaction that does not appeal to any producer is not lost. You replace it with a better offer: more of the same asset, or the same value in a different one. It is the mechanism Bitcoin users know as replace-by-fee, extended across assets.

Where the prices come from

A producer can write its price list by hand: an operator who prices the five assets they understand and ignores the rest runs a fully working node. Most will run a price server next to the node instead. It reads the market data sources the operator chose, applies the operator’s own admission rules, and publishes the list the node reads.

Go deeperWhat a price server checks, and why it is not part of the node

The admission rules are the operator’s: a minimum market capitalisation, a minimum daily volume, a maximum volatility, a discount for illiquid assets.

The price server is kept outside the node on purpose. It makes HTTP requests to third parties, holds API keys and parses JSON, and none of that belongs in the process that validates blocks.

Source: whitepaper, section 2.4. On the testnet, nodes read a public price server by default and an operator can run their own.

Why producers play along

Sequentia has no block subsidy. Nobody is paid in newly created coins for producing a block: producers earn the fees in it and nothing else. A producer that turns away dollars is turning away its own revenue.

It also carries the risk of what it accepts. Fees are paid through the block’s coinbase, which a producer cannot spend for 1,000 blocks, about sixteen hours and forty minutes. Whatever it took as a fee, it holds for that long. That is why a volatile or illiquid asset has to offer a little more than a liquid one: the price of the risk is set by the people who bear it.

Go deeperWhy the wait is 1,000 blocks

The period equals Bitcoin’s in clock time rather than in blocks: 1,000 blocks of sixty seconds are the same sixteen hours and forty minutes as Bitcoin’s 100 blocks of ten minutes. It serves two purposes.

  1. Safety. Until it has passed, the block that created the coinbase could still be undone by a Bitcoin reorganisation of the block it is anchored to, and anything that spent that coinbase would become permanently invalid.
  2. Incentive. A producer holds each fee asset for the whole period and bears its price risk.

Source: whitepaper, section 2.5.

And SEQ?

Sequentia has a token, SEQ, and it is not a gas token. Its one job is staking: it decides who may produce blocks. For fees it is treated like every other asset on the chain. It pays a fee only where a producer has priced it, on the same terms as dollars or gold, and a wallet never assumes it.

The supply is fixed at 400 million and no new SEQ is ever created, so holding it does not tax anyone who uses the chain.

Why this matters beyond convenience

A stablecoin that needs a second coin to move is a product with a dependency. Its users are exposed to that coin’s price, its congestion and its politics, and its issuer is building someone else’s network effect. Take the dependency away and there is very little left to recommend the chain the stablecoin happens to sit on today.

That is the claim: once dollars can move without a gas token, on a chain anchored to Bitcoin and traded directly against real bitcoin, there is no reason left to keep them anywhere else.

Try it

  1. Open the web wallet and take some test dollars (USDX) from the faucet. Take nothing else.
  2. Send some to a friend. The fee is charged in USDX.
  3. Take a second asset, send again, and choose which of the two pays the fee.
